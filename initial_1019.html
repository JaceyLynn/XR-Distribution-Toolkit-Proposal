What we're solving, and how

Three core problems, each answered directly by one of the three proposals below, all inside one product. Smaller points sit in a separate list because they become features of those solutions.

#Core problemWhoSolution
1Artists don't know where or how they could show their work, or what to plan for.
  • Distribution is an afterthought, so finished work doesn't fit venues (the 5×4 m example).
  • Venues feel like a closed shop (“what's the doorbell?”).
  • After a festival premiere there's no clear next step.
Meeting; report key questions; roundtables 1 and 3		 Artists, producers Proposal 1 · Guided journey. Develop → Premiere (skippable) → Circulate, one question at a time with relevant cases beside it. In Circulate the toolkit suggests venues from the answers, and the artist edits that list instead of naming venues themselves. Every venue shows its result details (description, how to reach out, website). The downloadable plan collects the answers, the kept venues and a “what to check with each venue” list.
2Finding venues that fit a work means wading through a big table or a diagram that's hard to read.
  • The Kumu diagram is hard to read for people who don't use diagrams.
  • The Airtable on its own is plain filtering.
Meeting		 Artists, curators Proposal 2 · Pathway. Step by step to a venue list, starting from one of three guiding questions (“Who shows work like mine?”, “I already have a format in mind”, “I want to show at a certain kind of venue”), with several choices allowed at each step. It ends in shortlist and compare: star venues, then see them side by side.
3Institutions and funders can't see that a working field exists.
Meeting (“put the field on a map”)		 Museums, funders, curators Proposal 3 · Atlas. A globe or map of every venue with a results list, with counts by region, type and format, a date stamp, and required and flexible filters ranked by drag and drop.

1

Guided journey

“Where is your project now? Let's think through where it could go, and you take the plan with you.”

Answers problem 1Artists don't know where or how they could show their work, or what to plan for.

Stages give the structure, and one question at a time fills each stage. There are no accounts and nothing is stored on a server. The plan builds up during one visit, and at the end, or at any point, the artist downloads it. Closing the tab clears it, and the page says so.

Sitemap: three stages, one plan
Stage 1

Develop

Production parametersAudience & impact

“What discipline?”, “What format?”, “Who is it for?”, “What's essential and what could change?”

Stage 2 · skippable

Premiere

Festivals“Is it ready, and at what level?”

One click to skip, for work going straight to venues. A skipped stage stays in the tab bar and can be reopened at any time.

Stage 3

Circulate

Venues & platformsDistributorsSpaces & platforms

Go direct or with support, and the region to show in. Then the toolkit suggests venues from those answers, so an artist who doesn't know where to start still gets a list; they keep or remove each one. A last set of prompts covers what to check with a venue (space, installation, staffing, fees, access).

The stages are a guide, not a fixed order: the artist can open any tab at any time and come back to earlier ones. Alongside the stages: the Cases & reports library, and About & data. The Extend stage from the earlier draft becomes reading material in the library.

Click through it: answer a question, set how fixed each answer is, skip or reopen Premiere, and open “Download plan” to see what the artist takes away. In Circulate, question 4 suggests real venues from the Airtable export based on the answers so far; remove any that don’t fit or ask for another. How each question type works
  • Choice questions (format, audience type, go direct or use a distributor) narrow the venue results.
  • Thought questions (what does success look like?) don't filter. The artist tags each one as required, flexible or to find out, with an optional short note.
  • Contact route comes from the Airtable field “How the venue curates” (open calls, by invitation, contact directly).
  • Gaps such as space size or tech specs, which the data doesn't hold, become “questions to ask this venue” in the plan.
What the download contains
  • The project's answers, grouped by stage.
  • Required / flexible / to find out lists.
  • Shortlisted venues and festivals with official links.
  • Questions to ask each venue, and the cases that were opened.
  • The date and data version, since venue records change.

A PDF reads best; Markdown or plain text is simplest to build.

2

The pathway

“Walk your project down the diagram, one layer at a time.”

Answers problem 2Finding venues that fit a work means wading through a big table or a diagram that's hard to read.

The Kumu diagram becomes the navigation. The artist starts by picking a guiding question: “Who shows work like mine?”, “I already have a format in mind” or “I want to show at a certain kind of venue”. Each question sets its own order of levels, and every route ends in real venues, each showing how to approach it, which can be starred, shortlisted and compared. A “consider” prompt at the bottom shows the question and case that matter at each step. Festivals sit beside the main path as an optional stage.

3

The atlas

“Here is the whole field. Look around.”

Answers problem 3Institutions and funders can't see that a working field exists.

The data is the home page. One screen: a globe (or flat map) of every record, a ranked filter rail, a results list underneath and a detail drawer. Each filter is either required or flexible, the same two words the Guided journey uses, and dragging filters up or down sets which ones count most. Guidance appears as small “consider this” cards. Venue networks draw as links between places. If the data stops being maintained, the atlas still works as a dated snapshot of the field. It is also where the other two proposals send people for results.

Tap a filter to change it, tap Required or Flexible to switch, and drag the ⠿ handle (or use the arrow keys on it) to rank. The matching venues on the right come best match first, and each one says why it appeared. Moving a flexible filter up or down re-ranks the list straight away, and venues that moved are marked with an arrow. The flat map is the demo view for now; Globe shows the earlier static preview. Venues are real records from the Airtable export; how each venue curates is not in the public view yet.

How the ranked filters work
  • Required filters hide every record that doesn't match. “It must be in Europe” hides everything outside Europe.
  • Flexible filters hide nothing. “Europe preferred” keeps other regions but lists European venues first. They rank what's left, so the best matches rise to the top of the list and stand out on the map.
  • Drag to rank: the higher a flexible filter sits, the more it counts. Moving Format above Region puts set-based venues anywhere ahead of other venues in Europe.
  • Every result says why it appeared (“2 of 2 required, 1 of 2 flexible”), which is one of the 12 November test checks.
How the other two proposals feed it
  • Guided journey: answers tagged required arrive as required filters and answers tagged flexible arrive as flexible ones. One set of words, one set of controls.
  • Pathway: the chosen layers arrive as required filters, which the artist can relax to flexible to see more venues.
  • The ranking lives in the page address, so a shared link keeps it, and it goes into the plan download.

Shared by all three proposals

The cheapest base to build on

All three proposals sit on one base: one place to edit the data, one set of data files, and one small web app. The research team keeps working in Airtable, which they already use. Nothing runs on a server, so there is nothing to pay for each month and nothing to maintain when nobody is working on it.

PartChoiceCost
Data editingThe existing Airtable base, with a few extra tables (support organisations, case studies, questions, networks) and map coordinates added once to each venue.Free plan, if the base stays within its record limit
Suggest a venueAn Airtable form that feeds a “to review” table. Nothing reaches the site until someone moves it across.Free
Getting data into the siteAn export that saves the base as data files in the code repository. For the prototype this is a download from Airtable whenever the data changes.Free
Renames and tag mappingOne mapping file turns Airtable tags into the app's filter names, so nothing in the base has to be renamed.Free
The web appOne static site. Filtering, ranking, shortlists and the plan download all happen in the visitor's browser. A shared filter engine powers all three ways in.Free
Map and globeOpen-source map tools with OpenStreetMap tiles, and open world outlines for the globe.Free, with credit shown
HostingA free static host such as GitHub Pages.Free; an own domain is optional, around the cost of a yearly domain name
Why this is the cheapest
  • No server, database or logins to run or secure.
  • The research team edits in a tool they already know.
  • Every proposal reuses the same data and the same filter engine, so building a second way in costs much less than the first.
What it can't do
  • Changes in Airtable appear on the site after the next export, not instantly.
  • Plans are saved by download, not stored online.
  • If the venue list grows past a few thousand records, the browser-side filtering would need another look.
